## Project: Electis, online CSE voting platform

`/projects/electis`

- **Title:** Electis, online CSE voting platform
- **Client label:** Electis
- **Short summary** (Portfolio card and Google): Redesigning how an election is set up on an online voting platform: from one long technical page to guided steps, with a design system behind it.
- **Tags:** Product design · Information architecture · Prototyping · User testing · Design system · Design QA

### Introduction (left column)

Setting up a CSE election is a legal process with many parameters, and on the platform it was one long technical page. As the only UX/UI designer in a team of three, I redesigned and restructured it from its architecture, so that users could set up a CSE election on their own.

Electis is an online voting platform built on open-source code, designed for transparent and secure elections in companies.

- **Role:** Product Designer
- **Team:** 1 Operations specialist · 1 Engineer · 1 Product Designer
- **Period:** 2023 – 2024
- **Platform:** Web
- **Products:** CSE elections

### What I did

- Redesigning and restructuring the platform from its architecture.
- Mapping the flows of each type of election.
- Prototyping and testing with the team and with users.
- Proposing features that simplify a complex product.
- Building the design system and handing it off.
- QA after every released version.

### Product decisions

- The same information, reorganized — Nothing was added to the setup; it was reorganized. One long technical page became clear groups (calendar, polling office, voters, candidates), each one a step in a sidebar that can be completed in any order.
- Always know what is missing — Every step shows its status, and a checklist next to the form spells out what is left before the election can be created.
- Legal first, then simple — The flow for CSE elections had to follow legal and procedural rules. The design work was making that compliance feel simple and transparent.

### 01 · The starting point

One page held every parameter of an election. The redesign splits it into steps you can follow and check.


### 02 · Mapping the flow

For CSE elections the flow had to comply with legal and procedural requirements while staying simple and transparent. Mapping it first showed where the product could guide instead of ask.


### 03 · The new setup

Prototyped and tested with the team and with users, then iterated release after release.


### 04 · Design system

A full library of components based on Material Design, built so the product can scale and the hand-off to the internal team is seamless.

## Project: Electis, client dashboard

`/projects/electis-client-dashboard`

- **Title:** Electis, client dashboard
- **Client label:** Electis
- **Short summary** (Portfolio card and Google): A client dashboard that guides HR teams through their CSE elections, from MVP to delivery.
- **Tags:** Product design · AI-assisted design · Automation · MVP to delivery

### Introduction (left column)

CSE elections used to be organized by exchanging emails, with an overloaded project manager on one side and confused clients on the other. The dashboard orders and simplifies the process for both: a personalized calendar, document templates turned into auto-completed tools inside the dashboard, legal steps turned into tasks, and a place for every document and answer.

The interface reuses the design system we already had for the voting platform, designed in Figma on top of Material Design.

My first project made with AI. I worked with AI almost 100% of the time and was in charge of executing the project from the MVP to the final delivery and its operation.

- **Role:** Product Designer, from MVP to delivery
- **Platform:** Web
- **Users:** HR teams organizing CSE elections · Electis operations team
- **Design system:** The voting platform’s system (Figma, based on Material Design)
- **Stack:** AI-assisted design and build · HubSpot CRM · Figma

### Product decisions

- A simpler architecture — With a small team and limited resources, I proposed solutions that simplified the product and even its architecture.
- The client in one place — The dashboard is connected to HubSpot, the CRM, so every contact with a client lives in a single place. The operations team has one place with all the client information.
- Automate the repetitive — Emails, reminders, templates and frequent questions stopped being manual work for the operations team.

## Project: AAR Papers

`/projects/aar-web-app`

- **Title:** AAR Papers
- **Client label:** 10 Grounds
- **Short summary** (Portfolio card and Google): A redesign of the system scholars use to submit proposals to the American Academy of Religion: a simpler submission flow, a Bootstrap 5 design system and a mobile version, without touching the architecture.
- **Tags:** UX/UI design · Redesign · Design system · Bootstrap 5 · Responsive

### Introduction (left column)

PAPERS is the system scholars use to submit paper and session proposals to the meetings of the American Academy of Religion. It worked, but it read like unstyled defaults: stacked buttons, long paragraphs and plain tables.

The brief at 10 Grounds was to improve the experience without touching the underlying architecture, on a short timeline and with little room for design complexity. So the redesign stays inside Bootstrap 5: I reorganized and restyled what was already there, focusing on the submission flow, and then adapted every screen to mobile.

The work covers both sides of the system: the people who submit proposals, and the chairs and steering committees who receive them. It runs through the flows to create a paper, a papers session or a roundtable, submit a proposal and search program units.

- **Role:** UX/UI Designer
- **Platform:** Web, desktop & mobile
- **Users:** Submitters · Chairs and steering committees
- **Flows:** Create paper · Paper session · Roundtable session · Submit proposal · Search units
- **Tools:** Figma · Bootstrap 5

### Design decisions

- Choose first — The three proposal types became side-by-side cards, each with an icon, a short description and one action, instead of three buttons separated by paragraphs.
- Status at a glance — Proposals are grouped as saved and submitted, with badges for type and status and icon actions, in place of a plain table of text links.
- Two views of your work — Tabs separate the proposals you own from the ones you take part in.
- Inside Bootstrap 5 — Every component is a Bootstrap 5 one with new color, spacing and type, so the redesign could be applied without rebuilding the front end.
- Review in one place — Chairs and steering members see their units and every proposal sent to them, with type, status and rating, and accept or reject from the proposal page.
- Mobile, screen by screen — Forms, modals and lists were adapted one by one, so submitting a proposal from a phone is a full flow, not a squeezed desktop page.

## Project: Chefy, AI cooking app

`/projects/ai-cooking-app`

- **Title:** Chefy, AI cooking app
- **Client label:** 10 Grounds
- **Short summary** (Portfolio card and Google): An AI cooking assistant that suggests meals from what is already in your kitchen. UX/UI design, naming, branding and design system for the MVP.
- **Tags:** UX/UI design · Design Sprint · Naming · Branding · Design system

### Introduction (left column)

College students and busy families let ingredients go to waste that could have been a meal. Chefy is an AI cooking assistant that starts from what is already in your kitchen and suggests real meals to make with it.

At 10 Grounds we started with a Design Sprint, working in Figma and Miro, to understand the problem and map the user journey before a single line of code was written. I was the UX/UI designer in a cross-functional team of designers, content specialists and AI developers. I was part of those sessions from the first day, and once the MVP was defined I designed for it: the naming, the branding and the design system the screens are built with.

- **Role:** UX/UI Designer
- **Category:** Food Technology & Artificial Intelligence
- **Platform:** iOS & Android
- **Tools:** Figma

### Product decisions

- Three features, not ten — The sprint narrowed the MVP to what would prove the idea: a pantry to track what you have, AI recipes from those ingredients, and a daily cooking word game.
- Your kitchen comes first — Onboarding asks for your appliances and gear before any recipe, so every suggestion is something you can actually make.
- Say it or type it — Ingredients can be dictated or typed. Listing what is in the fridge should be as quick as saying it out loud.
- Review before you cook — Ingredients, servings and cooking time are confirmed in two short steps, so the AI works from what you really have.
- A character, not a chatbot — The assistant has a name and a face. Talking to a small robot chef feels lighter than filling in a form.
- A reason to come back — You collect points as you use the app, and a daily word game adds a small habit around cooking at home.
